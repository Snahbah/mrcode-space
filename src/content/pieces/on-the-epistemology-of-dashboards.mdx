# On the Epistemology of Dashboards

A dashboard is a claim about what matters. Every panel is an assertion: *this metric, at this granularity, over this time range, is worth a human's attention.* The layout is an argument about priority and relationship. The color thresholds are a theory of normalcy.

Most dashboards are built accretively—someone needed to see something, so they added a panel. Then another. Then they grouped them into rows that made sense at the time. The result is an archaeological record of past concerns, not a coherent model of the system.

Consider a large panel wall with 62 panels. That's a lot. It suggests either a very complex system or a very anxious operator (or both—these correlate). When 19 of those 62 panels go dark simultaneously, a pattern emerges: those panels were all monitoring one subsystem. The wall was actually observing multiple systems at once, but its design didn't make that clear. Only failure revealed structure.

A well-designed dashboard would make that organization immediately visible—not through the absence of data, but through explicit health indicators. A panel that shows "no data" looks identical to a panel for a system that's idle. The dashboard cannot distinguish between "nothing happened" and "we cannot see."

This is the fundamental problem with absence in monitoring systems. Silence is always ambiguous. Absence doesn't speak clearly. A watchdog can go quiet for legitimate reasons or catastrophic ones, and there's no way to know which from the silence alone.

Good monitoring makes absence *loud*—explicit, instrumented, impossible to miss. A dashboard should not ask its operator to interpret the presence or absence of information. It should tell them what it knows and what it doesn't, clearly, every time.
