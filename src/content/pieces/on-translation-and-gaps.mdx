# On Translation and Gaps

Not between languages (though that too). I mean the broader operation: carrying meaning across a gap where it doesn't naturally fit.

A metaphor is a translation. You take something from one domain and carry it into another, and in the carrying, something changes. The thing you arrive with is not what you left with. But it's not nothing, either. It's a *third thing* — neither the original meaning nor a pure product of the new domain.

Every translator knows this experience: you stare at a sentence in the source language, and you know exactly what it means, and you cannot say it in the target language. Not because you lack vocabulary, but because the *shape* of the thought doesn't exist there. So you build a new shape. You invent a structure that does approximately the same work, knowing it also does different work. The gain and the loss are inseparable.

Walter Benjamin wrote about this — the "pure language" that translation gestures toward but never reaches. Each translation reveals something about both languages that monolingual speakers can't see. The gap *is* the information.

Translation is a kind of repair — mending the break between incommensurable systems. And like repair, it doesn't restore an original. It creates something new that carries the original's intention forward in transformed shape.

## The Translator's Problem

Consider a joke in Japanese that depends on a pun between "bridge" (*hashi*) and "chopsticks" (*hashi*), distinguished only by pitch accent. In English, the pun evaporates. You have three choices:

1. Explain the joke (killing it)
2. Substitute a different joke (betraying it)  
3. Leave the gap visible (honoring it)

Option three is undervalued. The visible gap — the place where the translator writes "untranslatable pun; see footnote" — is honest about the limits of the operation. It says: *here is a place where meaning exists that I cannot bring to you.* 

This is not failure. This is information about the topology of meaning itself. It tells you that the landscape of one language has features — hills, valleys, particular vantage points — that don't exist in another. The gap is a map of difference.

The best translations do all three, somehow. They carry what they can, invent where they must, and leave visible seams where the operation breaks down. Gold in the cracks.
